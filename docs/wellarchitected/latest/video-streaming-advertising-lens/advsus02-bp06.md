# ADVSUS02-BP06 Reduce per-auction network overhead by consolidating bid request fan-out and reusing transport connections
<a name="advsus02-bp06"></a>

 Programmatic advertising auctions generate network traffic that scales with both the number of concurrent users and the number of demand partners in each auction. How bid requests reach demand partners, and how you manage transport connections, determines whether per-auction network overhead grows with business volume or stays bounded.

 **Desired outcome:**
+  You have quantified the network overhead per auction, per impression, and per bid request across your advertising delivery stack and established baselines for improvement.
+  Your auction architecture is chosen deliberately based on your business model, with the sustainability implications of client-side compared to server-side bidding understood and measured rather than assumed.
+  Your transport connections to demand partners reuse TCP connections through pooling and HTTP/2 multiplexing, and reuse TLS state through session resumption at re-establishment, avoiding redundant handshake overhead for high-volume repeat traffic.

 **Common anti-patterns:**
+  Establishing new TLS connections for every bid request to the same demand partner rather than using connection pooling with HTTP/2 multiplexing or TLS session resumption, which generates redundant handshake overhead that accumulates at high query rates.
+  Adopting server-side bidding purely for cost or infrastructure reasons without measuring the revenue impact from reduced cookie synchronization, lower identity match rates, or supply path optimization pressure from demand partners.
+  Measuring data transfer sustainability only at the aggregate infrastructure level without normalizing to business value units makes it impossible to distinguish between traffic growth from business success and traffic growth from architectural inefficiency.

 **Benefits of establishing this best practice:**
+  Connection pooling and session resumption amortize the cost of TLS establishment across many bid requests to the same partner, rather than paying full handshake cost per request.
+  When auction orchestration runs server-side, the sender has direct visibility into connection behavior, retry patterns, and failure modes that are opaque when requests originate from distributed end-user devices.
+  Normalizing data transfer to per-auction, per-impression, and per-bid-request denominators allows architecture changes to be evaluated against a stable baseline, distinguishing genuine efficiency improvements from traffic volume changes.

 **Level of risk exposed if this best practice is not established:** Medium

## Implementation guidance
<a name="implementation-guidance"></a>

 The sustainability impact of an advertising delivery stack is driven by the volume of data moved per unit of business value. Data moved is an engineering proxy for carbon: it gives a fast, fine-grained efficiency signal. In programmatic advertising, the business value unit is the ad impression, but the network cost of producing one includes every bid request sent to demand partners during the auction, most of which don't win. Understanding where data transfer is amplified (and whether it can be bounded) requires architectural decisions about both how bid requests originate and how transport connections are managed.

 Where auction orchestration happens determines whether per-auction traffic can be bounded at all. In client-side header bidding, each viewer's browser contacts every demand partner directly, distributing bandwidth across end-user devices and the public internet with no single point at which the fan-out can be shaped. In server-side header bidding, the publisher's server issues bid requests on behalf of all viewers, concentrating that bandwidth on publisher infrastructure. The number of bid requests per auction is similar in both models, but the origin differs and only server-side gives one orchestrator the whole fan-out. Consolidation, traffic shaping, and selective suppression are possible only where a single orchestrator observes every request, and connection pooling and colocate network paths become available only there as well. Consolidation has revenue consequences: identity match rates and third-party cookie synchronization are typically lower server-side than client-side, which can depress CPMs, and running the infrastructure adds Multi-Region deployment, low-latency scaling, and timeout-budget management as publisher responsibilities. Most publishers settle on hybrid, keeping high-value partners client-side and routing overflow server-side. Reducing the distance and number of hops a packet travels is a core sustainability principle for network optimization, as outlined in the networking guidance of the [AWS Well-Architected Framework Sustainability Pillar](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/sustainability-pillar/sustainability-pillar.html).

 Transport connection management applies regardless of which auction architecture you choose. Bid request payloads differ by a large multiple across formats. A display request carries little beyond the basic impression and device context, while video and native requests add extensions, supply chain objects, and audience segments that can multiply the payload several times over. Measure your own distribution per format rather than assuming a figure, because the ratio between handshake and payload decides which optimization matters. For small display payloads, opening a fresh TLS connection is disproportionately expensive, because a full handshake with certificate exchange can move several times more bytes than the bid request itself. For larger video and native payloads, the handshake is only comparable in size. Either way, the question is whether high-QPS traffic to a partner re-pays that setup cost on every request or amortizes it across many.

 A persistent, multiplexed connection resolves this by carrying many concurrent requests over one already-established transport session, so the marginal per-request handshake overhead approaches zero once the connection exists. Sharing one connection also concentrates risk: when many streams share a single TCP connection, a lost packet stalls every stream on it until retransmission completes. This head-of-line blocking is benign for bulk web traffic but hazardous for real-time bidding, where the whole bid-response window is only a few hundred milliseconds and each bidder receives a fraction of it, so one stall can cascade into correlated timeouts across an auction. Minimize connections to reduce handshake overhead without making each connection a larger failure domain. Optimize payload size separately by format, since OpenRTB's verbose JSON adds more overhead to video and native requests than to already-compact display requests.

 A connection has a fixed setup cost in bytes and computation. Pay it once and reuse the resulting state across the repeat traffic that dominates any high-volume partner relationship. The payoff concentrates in the busiest partner relationships: attention on a partner reached a few times per auction returns little, while the same attention on top-QPS partners compounds. Partner capabilities are uneven. Support for transport version, stream multiplexing, payload compression, and reusable session state varies by partner, so no optimization applies uniformly across the demand stack. Because capabilities are uneven, efficiency has to be read against a stable business-value denominator rather than an aggregate byte count.

 **Note:** Track absolute data-transfer totals alongside the ratios for each unit, because efficiency gains for each impression can be offset by overall traffic growth. Reporting both the volume reduction and the cost reduction builds the business case for continued optimization.

### Implementation steps
<a name="implementation-steps"></a>

1.  **Establish per-auction, per-impression, and per-bid-request data transfer baselines:** Query your cost and usage data (CUR 2.0, available through [AWS Data Exports](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/cur/latest/userguide/what-is-data-exports.html)) to determine the total data transfer volume by type (internet egress, inter-region, intra-region), noting that it reports at service, Region, and Availability Zone (where populated) granularity, not per bid request, so derive the per-bid-request figure by allocating measured transfer across your bid-request counts rather than expecting a direct line item. Join this with application metrics (auction counts from your bidding server logs, impression counts from your ad server logs, and outbound bid request counts from your partner integration metrics) to calculate bytes-per-auction, bytes-per-impression, and bytes-per-bid-request. Each metric measures a different concern. Per-auction measures overall auction efficiency, per-impression normalizes to the business value unit and accounts for fill rate variations, and per-bid-request is the most sensitive indicator of transport-level optimizations such as connection pooling and session resumption. Use the [Data Transfer tab in the CUDOS dashboard](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/guidance/latest/cloud-intelligence-dashboards/cudos-cid-kpi.html) or [Amazon Athena queries against your CUR data](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/cur/latest/userguide/cur-query-athena.html) to identify the top resources and usage types contributing to data transfer.

1.  **Evaluate auction architecture choices against sustainability and revenue tradeoffs:** Document your current auction model (client-side, server-side, or hybrid) and measure the sustainability and revenue implications of each. For demand partners where cookie synchronization is critical to match rates, client-side bidding may produce higher CPMs that justify the end-user device bandwidth, while for partners with lower match rate sensitivity and less aggressive supply path preferences, server-side bidding consolidates traffic to publisher infrastructure where it can be pooled. If you operate server-side infrastructure, consider [Prebid Server on AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/solutions/guidance/deploying-a-prebid-server-on-aws/) for auction orchestration. For optimized connectivity to demand partners, evaluate [AWS RTB Fabric](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rtb-fabric/latest/userguide/what-is-rtb-fabric.html), which provides private network paths purpose-built for RTB workloads. Don't treat this as a universal choice between sustainability and revenue, because most publishers use hybrid models that balance match rate, supply path position, and operational complexity per demand partner.

1.  **Consolidate bid request fan-out through partner routing and throttling:** In server-side orchestrators, route bid requests selectively using per-partner win rate (wins per ten-thousand bid requests is a useful normalization), geography, device type, format support, and demand-partner capacity signals. Establish a win-rate floor below which a partner is throttled or dropped for that auction format. In hybrid architectures where auctions fan out through both client-side and server-side paths, deduplicate bid requests so the same partner isn't sent the same auction twice. [Prebid Server](https://docs.prebid.org/prebid-server/overview/prebid-server-overview.html) supports per-adapter configuration for inclusion rules and throttling. Track bid-request count per auction as an operational metric. Sustained increases without corresponding revenue gains indicate routing drift.

1.  **Enable HTTP/2 multiplexing and connection pooling to demand partners:** For repeat bid traffic to the same demand partner, configure your bidding client library to use [HTTP/2 with connection pooling](https://http2.github.io/faq/) rather than opening new connections per request. Default connection pool settings in most HTTP client libraries target general web traffic and are typically undersized for RTB workloads, so review your library's HTTP/2 connection pool configuration for common tuning points such as maximum idle connections per host, idle timeout duration, and maximum concurrent streams per connection. [RFC 9113 Section 9.1](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9113#section-9.1) states that clients shouldn't open more than one HTTP/2 connection to a given host and port pair. You can open multiple connections per high-volume partner (sized from your observed concurrency and validated against your timeout and churn metrics) as a deliberate deviation from that guidance, trading strict connection minimization for a reduced scope of impact from TCP-level head-of-line blocking, where a single packet loss stalls all streams on that connection. Monitor connection churn (new connections per minute) as an operational metric, since high churn indicates the pool is undersized or keep-alive is too short.

1.  **Configure TLS 1.3 session resumption for connection re-establishment:** Use [TLS 1.3 session resumption](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8446#section-2.2) when HTTP/2 connections close and must be re-established (because of network events, load balancer rotations, or keep-alive timeouts) to reduce both the bytes transferred and the computational cost of re-establishing the connection. TLS 1.3 session resumption uses a pre-shared key (delivered in NewSessionTicket messages from the prior session) to omit the certificate chain and skip certificate verification and signature operations during the handshake. Don't enable 0-RTT early data for bid request traffic, because 0-RTT data is replayable by design, and RTB bid requests are non-idempotent POST operations that can affect budget, pacing, and frequency capping. Verify which TLS version is actually negotiated per demand partner and fall back to TLS 1.2 session resumption where TLS 1.3 isn't supported. For same-region demand partners on AWS, evaluate [AWS PrivateLink](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/privatelink/what-is-privatelink.html) for general private connectivity or [AWS RTB Fabric](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rtb-fabric/latest/userguide/what-is-rtb-fabric.html) for RTB-purpose-built private paths with Availability Zone-affinity optimized routing, since both provide more consistent network performance than public internet paths.

1.  **Reduce per-request payload size through compression and schema pruning:** Enable HTTP content encoding (gzip or Brotli) on both request and response paths where demand partners support it. Text-based OpenRTB JSON compresses well, with the largest gains on verbose video and native payloads. Prune the OpenRTB schema per partner by removing fields the partner doesn't use. Most demand partners publish their supported fields in integration specifications. Where demand partners support binary serialization, evaluate the [IAB Tech Lab OpenRTB Protobuf representation](https://iabtechlab.com/standards/openrtb/), which yields smaller payloads than uncompressed JSON. The gap narrows once both formats are compressed, and some platforms layer vendor-specific extensions onto the OpenRTB Protobuf representation. Track average bid-request payload size per partner as an operational metric to detect regressions (new fields added without pruning, or compression disabled on a partner endpoint).

1.  **Monitor per-auction, per-impression, and per-bid-request efficiency as operational metrics:** Add all three metrics to your operational dashboard, tracked daily. Set alarms on each ratio to detect regressions, such as a new demand partner integration that disables connection reuse, a configuration change that disables TLS session resumption, or an architecture change that shifts the balance between client-side and server-side in a way that affects network efficiency. Use [Amazon CloudWatch](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudWatch/latest/monitoring/WhatIsCloudWatch.html) custom metrics to publish these ratios and [CloudWatch anomaly detection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudWatch/latest/monitoring/CloudWatch_Anomaly_Detection.html) to alert on deviations from the established baseline.
